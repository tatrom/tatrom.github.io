Is that unknown device on the Wi-Fi a hidden camera? How to tell from your iPhone

Updated 15 October 2026

Most unknown devices on a hotel or Airbnb network are harmless: a smart TV, a streaming stick, a speaker, a printer, the host's phone. A Wi-Fi camera often gives itself away by what it serves: video on the RTSP ports 554 or 8554, or a camera or recorder service on 37777 (Dahua), 34567 (many low-cost DVRs and cameras), 8800 or 9800 (V380), or the ONVIF ports 8899 and 2020. What an unknown device usually is

A device list from any network scanner, Argus included, shows every device that answers, and on a rental's network that is rarely just your phone and the router. Before you worry, account for what you can see in the room: TVs and streaming sticks, smart speakers, printers, thermostats, smart plugs and bulbs, a games console, the host's own devices and other guests' phones. Many of them never announce a name, so “unknown” on its own says very little. What matters is what the device does.

The ports that point to a camera

A camera exists to deliver video, and on a local network it does that through a small set of well-known services. Argus knocks on each of these ports on every device it finds — except the router and your own iPhone, which it labels for you — and rates the device by what answers:

High is strong evidence: go and find that device. A network video recorder can stream over RTSP too, and a recorder in a rental is worth finding just as much as a camera. A web page alone is a weaker hint, because routers, printers and smart-home hubs have one too, which is why Argus calls it Medium, and opening the address in Safari usually settles it in seconds. A device with no camera port whose own reply names it as a printer, a router, a TV or a speaker is rated Low and set aside. A device rated None can still be a camera that keeps its ports closed or records to a memory card, so the camera finder stays your second check.

Ask the device what it is

When a port answers, Argus reads the device's own reply — from an RTSP request on 554 and 8554, or from its web page on 80, 8080, 88, 81 or 8000 — and shows the server name, the login realm or the page title. That reply often names the firmware, which tells you far more than a port number does. Argus reads only the first few kilobytes, never follows the device anywhere else and never sends a password.

  1. Step 1 — Join the Wi-Fi the room uses

    Connect to the network the host or the hotel gave you. Argus can only see the network your iPhone is on, so a camera on a separate network will not show.

    Open the Wi-Fi scanner. The first time, iOS asks whether Argus may find devices on your local network: allow it, or iOS blocks the scan. The sweep checks every address in your iPhone's network range — the whole network on almost every home router — and usually takes under a minute.

  3. Step 3 — Set aside what you can explain

    Argus labels the router and your own iPhone. Match the rest against what you can see in the room. Devices the network cannot name, and any device that looks like a camera even when it has a name, are grouped as unidentified.

  4. Step 4 — Read each unidentified device

    For each one, look at its camera rating, its open ports and its own reply. If it has a web page (port 80, 443 or 8080), open its IP address in Safari while you are on the same Wi-Fi: the login page usually names the maker and the model. Do not try to sign in. From your second scan of the same network range onwards, devices that were not there before are marked New.

  5. Step 5 — Find the object

    A High rating means: go and find it. Look at anything facing the bed, the shower or the sofa — smoke detectors, clocks, chargers, the TV, vents — then turn the lights off and check it with the camera finder: a camera lens throws back a tight, bright pinpoint that stays in place as you move the phone.

  6. Step 6 — If it is a camera, document it and report it

    Do not touch it, unplug it or take it apart. Photograph it in place, report it to the host or the hotel and to the booking platform, and to the police if it points at a bed or a bathroom. If you feel unsafe, leave.

When the Wi-Fi scan cannot help

If the scan finds nothing but the router and your iPhone, the network is probably isolating guests from each other — common in hotels — and no Wi-Fi scan can see the other devices. The same goes for a camera on a separate network, one with its own SIM card, one that only records to a memory card and one that is switched off. How can I tell if an unknown device on Wi-Fi is a hidden camera?

Check what it serves. A device that answers on the RTSP video ports 554 or 8554, on a recorder or camera service port (37777 for Dahua, 34567 for many low-cost DVRs and cameras, 8800 and 9800 for V380) or on the ONVIF ports 8899 and 2020 is very likely a camera or a recorder. Port 8000 (Hikvision) or a web admin page alone is a weaker hint, because servers, routers and printers open them too; opening its address in Safari usually tells you what it is.
